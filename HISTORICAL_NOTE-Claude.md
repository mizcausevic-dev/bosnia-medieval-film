# Historical note: "Manuscript into place" (15 s)

## What the film says
On 29 August 1189, Ban Kulin of Bosnia wrote to Dubrovnik. He promised Dubrovnik's people safe passage and trade in his lands, and his help while they were there. The scribe was Radoje. The Slavic text is in Bosnian Cyrillic and a Latin text also exists. Three copies survive: one in Saint Petersburg and two in Dubrovnik. Which is the original is debated. The charter's place in national histories is contested.

## What the document is
- **Issuer and date:** Kulin, "ban of Bosnia" (ruled c. 1180 to 1204). The colophon, in Karavdić's rendering, gives 29 August 1189 and names the feast (the Beheading of John the Baptist).
- **Addressees:** knez Krvaš and the citizens of Dubrovnik.
- **Content:** friendship and peace; Dubrovnik people who travel and trade in his land are received without any compensation (*odšteta* in Karavdić's translation) being claimed from them, unless someone gives a gift of their own will; his officials will not use violence against them; he will give help and counsel while they stay; an oath on the Gospel closes it. The film leaves out the "no toll" reading that some summaries use, because the sources word this clause differently.
- **Scribe:** Radoje, "the ban's scribe" (the Old Slavic form may be plural, which Karavdić notes and leaves open).
- **Script and language:** the Slavic text is Cyrillic (Bosnian Cyrillic, *bosančica*). Karavdić argues the vernacular text was made from a Latin template.
- **Copies:** A in the Russian Academy of Sciences, Saint Petersburg; B and C in the Historical Archive in Dubrovnik. B is on a whole sheet. C is on a damaged sheet missing a lower corner, with the whole text present (Karavdić, Figs. 2 and 3, and her footnote 8).

## What is contested, and how the film treats it
- **Which copy is the original.** The Saint Petersburg copy is traditionally treated as the most authentic. Authors name the two Dubrovnik copies "older" and "younger" in opposite ways, and J. Vrana's 1955 study asks in its title whether the original survives. The film says only that it is debated.
- **Superlatives and ownership.** Sources call it the oldest preserved document in Old Bosnian, "Bosnia's first international treaty", or a "birth certificate". Karavdić notes that since the 1990s the charter has been used politically, producing interpretations that are not scholarly. The film makes none of these claims, names no nation or language as owner, and says its place in national histories is contested.
- **Historical Bosnia is not modern Bosnia and Herzegovina.** Kulin's Bosnia was centred on the upper and middle Bosna valley (with Usora). Usora and Soli were added in 1324 and Hum about 1326 (Wikipedia). The film shows no map and no borders, and does not use "Herzegovina".

## Labels for what is reconstructed
- **Generated:** the writing surface, the ink, the lighting, the three blank sheets. A tag on screen says "Generated surface and ink · English rendering of a published translation" for the whole film.
- **Not claimed:** the writing material of any copy (no source I read gives it for Saint Petersburg, and Karavdić calls the Dubrovnik copies "on paper"), the letterforms of any copy, or the real size or condition of any sheet beyond the one missing corner.
- **English lines on the sheet:** an abridged rendering of Karavdić's modern translation ("Ja, ban bosanski Kulin, obećavam tebi, kneže Krvašu, i svim građanima Dubrovačkim"). The title *kneže* is left out because the sources differ on translating it.

## Limits of this work
No critical edition or facsimile was consulted, so the wording of the original and the exact shape of the Dubrovnik copy's missing corner are not checked against a primary image (only a reproduction of the text block). The UNESCO page was blocked (HTTP 403) and no UNESCO figures are used. The missing corner is the lower right, confirmed on the reproduction in Karavdić's PDF (printed p. 128, Fig. 3), which shows the text block but not the sheet outline.
