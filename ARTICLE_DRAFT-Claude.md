## The short answer

On 29 August 1189, Ban Kulin of Bosnia issued a written pledge to the people of Dubrovnik. He promised them safe travel and trade in his lands, protection from his officials, and his help while they stayed. The scribe was Radoje. The Slavic text is in Bosnian Cyrillic, and a Latin text exists too. Three copies survive: one in Saint Petersburg and two in Dubrovnik. Which one is the original is debated.

That paragraph is the part I can stand behind. The rest of this piece shows how much evidence sits under each sentence, because a 15-second film has no room for footnotes, and the claims in it should survive a reader who checks.

## How I grade the claims

I use four labels throughout, the same ones as in the fact sheet I approved before any frame was animated.

- **Verified:** two or more independent sources agree, or the document's own colophon (as rendered by a scholar) states it.
- **Attributed:** one source says it, and I name that source.
- **Contested:** scholars or public narratives disagree.
- **Unverified:** it could not be confirmed, so the film does not say it.

## What the charter says, claim by claim

| Issued 29 August 1189 | Verified | The colophon, in Zenaida Karavdić's rendering, gives the day, month and year and names the feast of the Beheading of John the Baptist. Wikipedia, the Zemaljski muzej, RFE/RL and Ramić's abstract agree. |

## Three copies, no settled original

Copy A, in Saint Petersburg, is traditionally treated as the most authentic. Karavdić calls it that, then adds that dating and authenticity across the three copies are problematic. Authors also disagree on which Dubrovnik copy is older: the 1989 collection and Josip Vrana's earlier work label them in opposite ways, and the title of Vrana's 1955 study asks whether the original survives at all.

The two Dubrovnik copies differ physically, and Karavdić documents the difference. Copy B is on a whole sheet. Copy C is on a damaged sheet that lacks a lower corner, though the full text is present. Her Figure 3 reproduction agrees: the lines of text shorten toward the lower right. The reproduction shows the text block, not the sheet's outline, so we know that a corner is missing and not what its edge looks like.

## "Bosnia" in 1189 is not modern Bosnia and Herzegovina

I grade this one **Attributed**, because it rests on Wikipedia's account of the Banate of Bosnia. Kulin's Bosnia was centred on the upper and middle Bosna valley, with Usora. Usora and Soli were added in 1324 and Hum around 1326. Hum had its own lord in Kulin's time, Miroslav, who was Kulin's brother-in-law. The banate was a nominal vassal of Hungary. Bosnia became a kingdom in 1377.

This is why the film shows no map and no borders. A map would have implied a territory the sources do not support.

## What I left out, and why

- **Superlatives.** Sources call the charter the oldest preserved document in Old Bosnian, "Bosnia's first international treaty," or a "birth certificate." These are interpretations, and the film makes none of them. It says "from 1189."
- **Ownership.** Karavdić observes that since the 1990s the charter has been used for political ends, producing readings that are not scholarly. The film names no nation or language as its owner. Its end card says only that the charter's place in national histories is contested.
- **A writing material.** The surface is meant to look aged, and the first version looked like parchment. That implied a fact nobody had verified, and the review below caught it.

## How the film was checked

I approved the fact sheet and the wording rules before animation, and I declined a facsimile so the film would not imply it shows a real copy. Everything on screen is generated: the surface, the ink, the lighting. A tag says so for the whole 15 seconds, along with a second disclosure: the lines on the sheet are an English rendering of Karavdić's modern translation, abridged to leave out Krvaš's title.

A separate reviewer who saw only the fact sheet, the video and extracted frames found real problems in the first build:

- the end card credited the 1989 collection, which had only been seen cited inside Karavdić, not read;
- the third sheet showed a torn bottom edge, when the source describes one missing corner;
- the disclaimers sat on screen for about one second.

All three are fixed in the final film. The reviewer also asked whether "promise" softened an oath. It does not: Karavdić's translation reads *obećavam tebi*, "I promise you."

## Questions people ask

**When was the Charter of Ban Kulin written?**
On 29 August 1189, according to the colophon as rendered in Karavdić's study and every secondary source consulted. **Verified.**

**Who wrote it?**
A scribe named Radoje, identified as the ban's scribe. **Verified** by Karavdić and Wikipedia.

**Where are the surviving copies?**
One in the Russian Academy of Sciences in Saint Petersburg and two in the Historical Archive in Dubrovnik. **Verified.**

**Is the Saint Petersburg copy the original?**
It is traditionally treated as the most authentic, but the question is **contested**.

**Is it written in Cyrillic?**
The Slavic text is in Bosnian Cyrillic (*bosančica*), and a Latin text exists as well. **Verified.**

**Was Kulin's Bosnia the same as modern Bosnia and Herzegovina?**
No. It was centred on the upper and middle Bosna valley, and other regions joined later. **Attributed** to Wikipedia.

## Limits of this piece

No critical edition or facsimile was consulted. The wording of the original, and the exact shape of the Dubrovnik copy's missing corner, are not checked against a primary image. The wording of the 1989 collection *Osamsto godina Povelje bosanskog bana Kulina 1189–1989* (ANUBiH) is cited in Karavdić and not read here, so the next step for anyone writing about this seriously is to read it. The UNESCO page was not reachable when it was checked, so the film and this piece use no UNESCO figures. Treat the Wikipedia-sourced points (reign dates, the banate's territory) as secondary, and check them before you cite them elsewhere.
